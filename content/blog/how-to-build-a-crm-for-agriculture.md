An agriculture CRM helps agribusinesses keep track of farmers, buyers and field activity in one place. It differs from a standard CRM because of the context: farmers may have limited connectivity, field agents work on low-cost phones, and relationships are tied to seasons, crops and land. This guide covers who needs one, the features to build first, why offline-first design matters, and how to scope an MVP.

## Who needs an agriculture CRM?
* Input suppliers selling seed, fertilizer and equipment
* Aggregators and buyers who source produce from many farmers
* Cooperatives and outgrower schemes
* Agri-fintech companies and lenders
* NGOs and extension programs working with farmer groups

If you manage hundreds or thousands of farmers on spreadsheets and WhatsApp groups, you already feel the problem: duplicated names, lost records and no clear view of who has received what.

## What it should track
* **Farmers and farms:** names, contact details, farm location, size and crops
* **Field activity:** visits, advice given, photos and notes
* **Inputs and credit:** what was supplied, what is owed and what has been repaid
* **Produce:** expected and delivered volumes, quality and prices
* **Buyers and orders:** who wants what, and when
* **Payments:** amounts paid, dates and methods
* **Training and groups:** attendance and membership

## Must-have features for version one

| Feature | Why it matters |
| :--- | :--- |
| Farmer and farm profiles, with location | The single source of truth for every farmer |
| A mobile app for field agents that works offline | Agents are often out of signal |
| Visit and activity logging with photos | Proof of work and a record of advice given |
| Crop and season records | Everything in agriculture is tied to the season |
| Input and loan tracking | Protects your money and the farmer's record |
| Produce collection records | Shows what was bought from whom and at what price |
| Buyer and order management | Connects you to demand |
| Roles and permissions | Agents, supervisors and managers see different things |
| Dashboards and reports | Shows the whole operation at a glance |
| Import and export with spreadsheets | Lets you bring in existing data |

## Offline-first design
Offline-first means the app works fully without a connection, stores data on the phone, and sends it to the server when a signal returns. This is the most important design decision for an agriculture CRM. It also means thinking about conflicts: what happens if two people edit the same farmer record while offline? Decide the rules early, keep the app light for low-cost Android phones, and test in real field conditions, not only in the office.

## Reaching farmers who do not use smartphones
Many farmers do not use smartphones or apps. Field agents are often the link. You can also reach farmers through SMS, WhatsApp or voice calls, and, depending on your market, other channels. Keep interfaces simple, use plain language, and consider local languages. The CRM does not need every farmer to log in. It needs good records and good ways to reach them.

## Data, consent and privacy
Farmer records include personal data and often the location of land. Collect only what you need, get clear consent, explain how the data will be used and who can see it, protect it with roles and backups, and follow data protection law. Decide who owns the data, especially when several organizations work together.

## Features to leave for later
* Satellite and weather data
* Automatic credit scoring
* Marketplace features for buyers and farmers
* Integrations with accounting, mobile money or logistics systems
* Advanced analytics and forecasting
* A farmer-facing app

## How to build it, step by step
1. Pick one value chain and one program, for example one crop and one region.
2. Run a Discovery Sprint. Map how data flows today, design the screens and get a fixed quote.
3. Test with field agents. Their feedback matters more than anyone's.
4. Build the agent app and the web dashboard first.
5. Pilot with a small group through one season stage.
6. Train the agents, and measure adoption as carefully as technical performance.
7. Expand to more crops, regions and features.

## How much does it cost?
A focused agriculture CRM, with an offline field app and a web dashboard, typically sits in the standard MVP range. Costs rise with the number of user roles, integrations, languages and reports, and with the amount of existing data you need to bring in. ## Common mistakes
* Designing for the office, not the field
* Ignoring offline use
* Importing messy data without cleaning it first
* Forgetting to train field agents
* Trying to cover every crop and region in version one

## Frequently asked questions
**Should I use an existing CRM or build a custom one?** If your process is ordinary sales and contact management, an existing tool may work. If you need offline field work, farm records, input tracking and produce collection, a custom system is often a better fit. Compare both during discovery.

**How long does it take to build?** A focused MVP typically takes 8 to 12 weeks. The timeline is confirmed in a fixed quote after discovery.

**Can farmers use it directly?** Not in version one, usually. Field agents use the app, and farmers are reached through simpler channels such as SMS or WhatsApp.

**What about farmers with no phone numbers or IDs?** Design for it. Use group leaders, agents and unique record numbers, and be careful with duplicates.
